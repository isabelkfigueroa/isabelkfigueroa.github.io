Incidents in the Life of Slave Girl, the 1861 autobiography by Harriet Jacobs, highlights the psychological abuse that enslavers inflict upon enslaved people. As a formerly enslaved woman from North Carolina, Harriet Jacobs exposes the manipulative behavior of her own enslavers, Dr. and Mrs. Flint, as well as others. Jacobs explains how in addition to the law and physical violence, the oppressors in her life use lies to promote their own agendas. One way the oppressor class lies is through its declaration of a false hierarchy in which the free white people are inherently superior to the enslaved black people. The people with power in Jacobs’s world profess to be superior because of God’s will, their better abilities, and their greater humanity. The people with power portray enslaved black people to be inevitable objects of subjugation because of their lower status in God’s eyes, and because of their incompetent and even subhuman nature. Another way the oppressors try to manipulate their victims is through professions of closeness. Jacobs highlights the words of the enslavers Dr. Flint and Mr. Sands as the men purport to treat Jacobs and other members of her enslaved family as family members of their own. Finally, people like Dr. and Mrs. Flint twist reality when they portray themselves as victims of the institution of slavery. 

## Superiors

Enslavers consistently try to tell enslaved people that enslavers are superior in terms of their Christianity, basic ability to survive, and humanity. As Jacobs relates, local slaveholders agree that one way they can try to justify slavery is through religion. They arrange for an Episcopal clergyman, Reverend Mr. Pike, to lead church services for enslaved people. Reverend Pike preaches to Jacobs and other enslaved people: “You must forsake your sinful ways, and be faithful servants. Obey your old master and young master - your old mistress and your young mistress. If you disobey your earthly master, you offend your heavenly Master. You must obey God’s commandments” (p. 48). The Christian minister tells the enslaved people that they must abandon any thoughts they may have of a different life, and be loyal slaves. He commands them to comply with their white enslavers - whether the enslaver is a man or a boy, a woman or a girl. According to Reverend Pike, slaves who displease their masters also displease God. The Christian minister spreads the message to the enslaved people that it is God’s will that some people are masters and others are slaves, and that enslaved people must accept God’s will. One slaveholder tells Jacobs another lie when he tells Jacobs about her escaped friend. Jacobs relates: “A slaveholder once told me that he had seen a runaway friend of mine in New York, and that she besought him to take her back to her master, for she was literally dying of starvation, that many days she had only once cold potato to eat, and at other times could get nothing at all…The whole story was false” (p. 38). The man lies to Jacobs in hopes that she and the other remaining enslaved people believe that freedom in the North is even worse than enslavement in the South. The man tells Jacobs that her friend begs to return to slavery because she lives miserably, with no food to eat at all. The lie suggests that unlike free white people, formerly enslaved black people are completely incapable of survival on their own. When Jacobs runs away from Dr. and Mrs. Flint, they imprison her children, in hopes that Jacobs hears this news and returns. Mrs. Flint complains about Jacobs to another enslaved person: “As for their mother, her ladyship will find out yet what she gets by running away. [Jacobs] hasn’t so much feeling for her children as a cow has for its calf. If she had, she would have come back long ago, to get them out of jail, and save all this expense and trouble” (p. 71). Mrs. Flint mocks Jacobs when she refers to Jacobs as “her ladyship,” and communicates that she really believes Jacobs has less of a maternal instinct than a “cow.” Mrs. Flint claims that Jacobs does not return because she lacks motherly feeling, and blames Jacobs for the imprisonment of the children. Mrs. Flint suggests that she knows how true women and mothers behave, and that Jacobs is beneath an animal - when in reality Mrs. Flint is the one who puts innocent children in jail, not Jacobs. When enslavers tell the people they enslave that God intends for them to be slaves, that they are incapable of self-sufficiency, and lower even than animals, they hope that their lies provide sound reasoning for the subjugation of a group of people.

## Equals

While sometimes enslavers try to belittle and dehumanize enslaved people, at other times, they try to convince enslaved people of the bond they share. Dr. Flint begins to make sexual advances towards Jacobs when she is sixteen. He tells her, “Did I not take you into the house, and make you the companion of my own children? Have I ever treated you like a negro? I have never allowed you to be punished, not even to please your mistress. And this is the recompense I get, you ungrateful girl!” (p. 30). Dr. Flint tries to tell Jacobs that he treats her like a white woman who is part of his family, not a black, enslaved woman who is part of his property. First, Dr. Flint says that he deserves thanks because Jacobs lives in his house. Dr. Flint argues that he does not force Jacobs to do hard labor in the fields the way other enslaved people must do. Second, he tells her he does not treat her like a black woman, he treats her like a white woman, which implies that he treats her with respect. Third, Dr. Flint claims that he does not allow other white people to physically hurt Jacobs. He insinuates that he is the only person who stands between her and the white people on the property, including his wife, that wish to harm her. Dr. Flint expects Jacobs, an enslaved woman on his property, to be thankful towards him. He tries to make Jacobs feel guilty, as though they have a romantic relationship where he gives her all he can, and she gives nothing in return. Later, Jacobs escapes to the north, and receives a letter from Dr. Flint in which he tells Jacobs of her Aunt Nancy’s death: “Could you have seen us round [your aunt’s] death bed, with her mother, all mingling our tears in one common stream, you would have thought the same heartfelt tie existed between a master and his servant, as between a mother and her child” (p. 166). In the picture that Dr. Flint paints, he blends his own family and Jacobs’s family together. He depicts the enslavers and the enslaved side by side as they mourn a shared loved one. Dr. Flint claims that he and Nancy’s mother share the same grief over Nancy. He insists that “the same heartfelt tie” exists between himself the enslaver and Nancy the enslaved person (although he does not say “slave,” but uses the euphemism “servant”), and between real family members. When Jacobs’s brother William escapes, his enslaver, Mr. Sands, complains: “I trusted [William] as if he were my own brother, and treated him as kindly. The abolitionists talked to him in several places; but I had no idea they could tempt him. However, I don’t blame William. He’s young and inconsiderate, and those Northern rascals decoyed him” (p. 129). Mr. Sands describes William as his brother. He mentions the trust and kindness he affords William. Mr. Sands expresses surprise that William leaves him when the two are in New York and William has the chance. He desrcribes the abolitionists and “Northern rascals” as villainous people who “tempt” and “decoy” young people. The enslavers Dr. Flint and Mr. Sands claim to treat some of their enslaved people as fellow white people, as truly loved ones, and as brothers in an attempt to gain familial compassion from the people they exploit.

## Victims

At times, the enslavers in Jacobs’s life go so far as to portray themselves as victims in the slavery-based society. Jacobs knows that Dr. Flint is the father of at least eleven enslaved children on the property. If his enslaved children or their mothers draw attention, Dr. Flint sells them, and effectively makes them disappear. Jacobs recounts the experience of one enslaved woman who reveals the identity of her new baby’s father. Jacobs writes, “...Dr. Flint handed [the woman and baby] both over to a slave-trader. The guilty man put their value into his pocket, and had the satisfaction of knowing that they were out of sight and hearing…She had forgotten that it was a crime for a slave to tell who was the father of her child” (p. 10). Dr. Flint rapes an enslaved woman on his property and fathers a child by her. The community accepts Dr. Flint’s behavior while it remains unaddressed. When the enslaved woman identifies the Dr. Flint as the child’s father and the information spreads, the community becomes uncomfortable with the spoken truth. The enslaved woman’s words embarrass Dr. Flint and the community. Dr. Flint immediately expels the woman and his own child via their sale. He collects not only a sum of money for the woman and his child, but also makes them both effectively disappear forever. The events reveal how in Jacobs’s world, the community does not regard the enslaved woman as a victim. The community regards her accusation as more of an affront to society than Dr. Flint’s behavior. Dr. Flint punishes the woman for her social crime. When Mrs. Flint learns of Dr. Flint's harassment of Jacobs, Mrs. Flint confronts Jacobs and learns the truth. Jacobs relates: “As I went on with my account [Mrs. Flint’s] color changed frequently, she wept, and sometimes groaned. She spoke in tones so sad, that I was touched by her grief…She felt that her marriage vows were desecrated, her dignity insulted; but she had no compassion for the poor victim of her husband’s perfidy. She pitied herself as a martyr…” (p. 28). As Mrs. Flint learns the details of her husband’s sexual harassment of a teenager, she weeps for herself because she feels that the most important result of her husband’s behavior is the disrespect to her and her marriage. Mrs. Flint does not concern herself with Jacobs, the enslaved teenage girl and victim of Dr. Flint’s harassment. Mrs. Flint sees Jacobs not as a powerless child, but as a woman who shares in Dr. Flint’s disrespect of her. When Jacobs’s Aunt Nancy dies, Mrs. Flint wants to bury Nancy at the foot of the plot where Mrs. Flint plans to rest when she dies. When Nancy’s mother requests that her daughter’s body return to her, “Mrs. Flint graciously complied with her wish, though she said it was painful to her to have Nancy buried away from _her_. She might have added with touching pathos, ‘I was so long _used_ to sleep with her lying near me, on the entry floor’” (p. 140). Mrs. Flint claims that the request to relinquish Nancy’s body causes her “pain.” She focuses entirely on her own imagined closeness with her deceased slave, and not on Nancy’s real family ties. Mrs. Flint regards her surrender of Nancy as a great gift or accommodation on her part and most likely counts her decision about Nancy as a favor to Nancy’s family, at the expense of her own comfort. Jacobs relates examples enslavers who view themselves as victims, Dr. Flint believes that an enslaved woman damages his reputation, Mrs. Flint believes Jacobs contributes to the deterioration of her marriage, and believes that Jacobs’s family asks a lot of her.

Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl reveals how oppressors will say anything to keep themselves advantaged.
