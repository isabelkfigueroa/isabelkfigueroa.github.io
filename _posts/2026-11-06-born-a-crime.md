The title of Trevor Noah’s 2016 memoir, _Born a Crime_, prepares readers for a world full of the unexpected. How can someone be born a crime? Noah explains that his father is white and his mother is black. In 1980s South Africa, the law disallows relationships between white people and black people. Thus, Noah enters the world as both an innocent baby and the evidence of a crime. Throughout the book, Noah continues to surprise his readers with unpredictable anecdotes. The situations Noah describes serve not only to entertain the reader, but also to illustrate the complexity of Noah’s own life and of South Africa generally. In 1980s South Africa, a white minority controls the country. The white minority group divides and suppresses the predominantly black population, the mixed race Coloured population, and other racial groups. In a world where the laws and accepted practices make no sense, Noah seeks to challenge his readers’ notion of good and bad. Noah highlights individuals whose lives sometimes conflict with the law. He shows how people can retain their humanity and goodness while they break the law and even live consistently outside of it. Conversely, Noah describes circumstances that appear agreeable on the surface, yet prove to be far worse than they seem. Finally, Noah shares how some situations can reflect two very different realities at the same time. In Born a Crime, Trevor Noah challenges his readers’ expectations.

# Better

Noah humanizes people who find themselves in sex work, organized crime, or jail. Noah relates how sex workers help his mother live in Johannesburg at a time when her residence in the city is illegal. Noah explains:  “...[my mother] learned the rules of navigating the city from other black women who had contrived to live there: prostitutes. Many of the prostitutes in town were Xhosa. They spoke my mother’s language and showed her how to survive. They taught her how to dress up in a pair of maid’s overalls to move around the city without being questioned” (p. 24). Noah refers to a traditionally marginalized and disparaged group of people and highlights the critical aid that these women provide in his mother’s life. Noah portrays the women not as objects on the fringes of society, but as the trail-blazing mentors who help his mother defy apartheid. He emphasizes their humanity when he explains that many of the sex workers in Johannesburg come from the same ethnic and language background as his mother. Noah highlights the humanity of another group of people outside the legal economy when he talks about gangsters. For a few years after high school, Noah sells pirated CDs with his friends in the Alexandra township. He says, “In the hood, gangsters were your friends and neighbors. You knew them. You talked to them on the corner, you saw them at parties…The hood made me realize that crime succeeds because crime does the one thing the government doesn’t do: crime cares. Crime is grassroots. Crime looks for the young kids who need support and a lifting hand” (p. 209). Noah claims that gangsters are sometimes people that he knows and likes, familiar faces that he sees everyday. He explains that in places like the Alexandra township, gangsters often provide goods and services that are otherwise not accessible for many of the people there. Again, Noah shares a different perspective of a group that society generally vilifies. Noah spends some time in jail himself, and recounts his interaction with a fellow jailmate. He recalls: “On the third day, the cops brought in the largest man I’d ever seen. This guy was huge. Giant muscles. Dark skin. Hardened face. He looked like he could kill all of us…everyone was terrified…Once we started talking I realized he wasn’t the Hulk at all. He was the sweetest man, a gentle giant, the biggest teddy bear in the world…He was out of work and needed money to send to his family back home” (pp. 236-237). Like all the others in the jail, Noah assumes the worst of the new arrival. The man’s size: “large, huge, giant,” intimidates the other men in the jail. Despite the man’s physical size, he is vulnerable. He does not want to kill anyone, he wants to provide for his family. Noah empathizes with the man’s life story and realizes that the man is nothing like what he seems. Noah relates the stories of sex workers, gangsters, and men in jail, to show that these people can also be mentors, friends, and family men.

# Worse

Noah also shows how some situations may seem positive on the surface, but can harmful in less visible ways. Noah claims: “The triumph of democracy over apartheid is sometimes called the Bloodless Revolution. It is called that because very little white blood was spilled. Black blood ran in the streets” (p. 12). Noah highlights the popular term, the “Bloodless Revolution,” that refers to the government transition from apartheid to a multiracial democracy. The term “Bloodless Revolution” carries a positive, nonviolent connotation. Noah challenges this narrative when he asserts that “Black blood ran in the streets.” Although the political class in South Africa, and those national leaders most visible to the rest of the world, experience a relatively smooth change, the rest of the country undergoes turmoil. Noah also describes a time in his personal life that is not as ideal as it seems. As the son of a white man and a black woman, Noah is mixed-race and looks like he could be part of the Coloured community in South Africa. However, Noah is not a part of this specific multiracial, cultural group. He describes his experience when he moves to a Coloured neighborhood: “In Eden Park, everyone looked like me…The animosity I felt from the colored people I encountered growing up was one of the hardest things I’ve ever had to deal with. It taught me that it is easier to be an insider as an outsider than to be an outsider as an insider” (p.117). In a white environment, Noah stands out as a person of color. In a black neighborhood, people recognize whiteness in Noah’s background. In a Coloured community, Noah should fit right in. While his appearance blends in with those around him, Noah’s language and cultural background is different. He learns that in a Coloured community, where he looks Coloured, people are actually most suspicious of him. Within his own family, Noah relates his experience with his step-father: “Abie was charming and hilarious and had an easy, gracious smile. He loved helping people, too, especially anyone in distress. If someone’s car broke down on the freeway, he pulled over to see what he could do…the old lady next door needed help moving boxes? He’s that guy. He liked to be liked by the world, which made his abuse even harder to deal with” (p. 248). Noah first communicates to his readers the person his stepfather seems to be: “charming, hilarious, gracious,” and quick to help strangers and neighbors alike. Then, Noah reveals the abuse that his stepfather commits. Noah discusses the “Bloodless Revolution,” his experience in a neighborhood where he looks like everyone else, and the addition of a stepfather to his family as examples of situations that have negative qualities that are not obvious on the surface.

# Both

Other times, Noah describes situations that demonstrate two very different realities at once. The situations are neither better nor worse than they seem, but exhibit conflicting characteristics. Noah says of the black township Soweto, for example: “There is something magical about Soweto. Yes, it was a prison designed by our oppressors, but it also gave us a sense of self determination and control. Soweto was ours” (p. 40). Noah points out that Soweto is a ghetto where the powerful white minority of South Africa forces black people to live. The township exists because of racial segregation and injustice. At the same time, Noah explains that Soweto harbors something special. The people of the township are proud of what good they are able to create despite the injustice that surrounds them. Noah also shares about his complicated relationships with his parents. Noah says: “My father is a complete mystery” (p. 103). On the one hand, as the only child of Patricia and Robert, Noah’s parents are his closest DNA relatives. Noah grows up with a knowledge and appreciation of his father. On the other hand, Noah’s father is utterly unknown to him. Noah’s father is both one of the people closest to him, and someone he understands very little. As a child, Noah lives with his mother, Patricia. When Patricia suffers gunshot wounds at the hands of her ex-husband, Noah stays by her side at the hospital. Noah remembers: “We sat there and she squeezed my hand and we cracked each other up the way we always did, mother and son, laughing together through the pain in an intensive-care recovery room…” (p. 281). Hard laughter seems unexpected between two people in the hospital after a traumatic incident of domestic violence. Noah does not intend to lessen the devastation of the event with the joking and laughing. Instead, he simply illustrates how the most unlikely realities can unfold at the same time. He shows how a mother and son can rely on their enduring relationship to bring familiarity and levity to a highly unusual and stressful time. Noah refers to his experience with Soweto, his father, and his mother to reflect on the fact that some realities do exist neatly as one thing or the other.

The memoir title, _Born a Crime_, prepares readers for Trevor Noah’s understanding of life. In the South Africa of Noah’s childhood and young adulthood, life exists within the context of Apartheid and its legacy. In this context, a white minority enjoys privileges over the black majority and other racial groups. People must navigate a world where injustice reigns. Noah recognizes the unjust context of his country at an early age. This understanding allows him to approach people, events, and situations, with a critical eye. Noah’s independent, critical thinking enables him to move past stereotypes. When he considers specific sex workers, career gangsters and petty criminals, he does not allow their marginalized social positions to define them. He thinks about their situations, intentions and actions, and is able to see the good in people that others may not. Noah also understands that bad can exist within situations that seem good. He cites South Africa’s government transition around the 1990s, his experience in a place where he looks like everyone else but is not, and his family’s addition of a stepfather as events that are more harmful than they seem on the outside. Throughout the memoir, Noah describes realities that exhibit great contrasts at the same time. Noah highlights the suppression and resilience within Soweto and his close yet mysterious relationship with his father. Noah’s memoir ends with a scene of Noah and his mother as they share a laugh in his mother’s hospital room, which exemplifies the happiness and sadness that can exist simultaneously at any moment between a parent and child. _Born a Crime_ challenges readers to think deeply.
