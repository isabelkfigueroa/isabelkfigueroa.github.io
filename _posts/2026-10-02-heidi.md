Johanna Spyri’s 1880 novel, _Heidi_, follows the young girl Heidi as she moves to the mountains in Switzerland and begins to see the natural world come alive. At her new home, Heidi gets to know her grandfather and Peter, a boy who cares for local goats. Heidi also becomes more familiar with other entities, such as the sun, the mountains, and the winds. Heidi personifies these characters immediately, which illustrates her multifaceted understanding of them, and also their important role in her life. Heidi regards the sun as capable of interaction with her. In Heidi’s mind, the sun can form opinions and express itself. When Heidi looks at the mountains, she sees unique individuals. She recognizes the faces of her friends in the mountains, and likes to call the mountains by name. Heidi also recognizes different winds. The winds become active participants in Heidi’s life. They assume various roles as performers, farmers, or gracious hosts. Heidi views elements of the natural world more like fellow people because of how meaningful they are to her.

## Mountains

To Heidi, the mountains behave like real people. On her first full day in the mountains, Heidi joins Peter in the pasture where the goats wander all day. While Peter naps and the goats graze, “Heidi sat quite still, enjoying it all. She gazed so intently at the mountain peaks that soon they seemed to her to have faces and to be looking at her…” (p. 30). Heidi pays careful attention to her surroundings. Under Heidi’s close observation, the mountains seem to come alive. The mountains develop “faces,” and appear to her like new people. Furthermore, the mountains seem to “look” back at her, as if capable of a reciprocal relationship. That evening, Heidi’s grandfather tells her the two mountains are called Falkniss and Scesaplana, and that the beautiful sunset everyday is especially for them. He says: “[The sun] spreads that beautiful light over them so that they won’t forget him till he comes back in the morning” (p. 40). Heidi and her grandfather view the mountains as fickle twins who may “forget” their friends overnight, unless they receive a parting gift at the end of each day. Heidi spends a period of time in Germany, but when she returns to the mountains in Switzerland, she recognizes the familiar mountains. Happily, she believes they remember her as well: “Heidi looked around with growing delight at the mountain peaks she knew so well and which seemed to greet her like old friends.” (p. 158). After some time away, Heidi still feels as though she knows the mountains well. When she returns to Switzerland, Heidi feels that the same mutual connection exists between herself and the mountains as when she first saw them. The mountains “greet her,” and seem to welcome her home. Heidi compares the mountains to “old friends.” In Heidi’s world, the mountains can look at, forget, and greet others just like human neighbors.

## Sun

According to Heidi, the sun’s most human quality is its ability to express itself. The first morning Heidi spends at her grandfather’s house, her grandfather tells her she can go up to the pasture with Peter. He says that first, however, Heidi must wash her face, or the sun will laugh. Heidi cleans her face with water and tells her grandfather: “The sun can’t laugh at me now” (p. 28). Both Heidi and her grandfather portray the sun as a sentient being in their lives. They talk about the sun as if it can see them, and as if the sun judges their appearance and behavior. Heidi and her grandfather agree that the sun may “laugh” to see Heidi unkempt, but will approve of her washed face. When Heidi is away in Germany, she feels homesick for Switzerland. She finally confesses, “I want to go home because while I’m here Snowflake will be crying, and Grannie will be missing me too. And here I can’t see the sun saying goodnight to the mountains” (p. 104). Heidi wants to go back to Switzerland to see the goat, snowflake, Peter’s grandmother, Grannie, and the sun. Heidi considers the sun just as important as people and animals. She wants to see the sun “say goodnight” to the mountains again. When Heidi does return to Switzerland, the sun communicates with Heidi: “A sunbeam shone straight through the open window and seemed to be calling to her to come out” (p. 193). This morning, the sun “calls her to come out,” like a friend who beckons another to join him outside to play. For Heidi, the sun laughs, says ‘goodnight,’ and calls her to play, like a human friend. 

## Wind

Heidi considers the winds to exhibit a range of human activity. In the autumn: “...what [Heidi] liked most of all was the noise the wind made in the old fir trees. She often left what she was doing to go and stand under them with her face turned up, listening and watching the swaying branches as the wind whistled and whirled through them…” (p. 42). Heidi listens and watches the wind as if it is a free spirited performer. She listens to the wind “whistle,” like a human whistles a tune, and she admires the sound. She watches the way the wind “whirls” through the trees like a dancer. One Spring, Heidi notices different winds: “The gay young winds of spring blew over the tops of the fir trees, carrying off showers of old needles to make room for the new growth” (p. 233). The “gay young winds” that Heidi notices in the Spring are happy and youthful, as if winds can have emotions and ages like people do. They also seem to be consciously at work, when they remove old needles on the trees, just like farmers are busy in the Spring. When Clara and her grandmother come to visit, the wind attempts to serve as a good host alongside Heidi and her grandfather. When the friends eat a meal outdoors, “A gentle breeze fanned them as they sat at table, and rustled in the trees, making agreeable music” (p. 245). Heidi appreciates this wind that appears because it helps to make her guests comfortable. The wind chooses to blow mildly, in a way that “fans” the people outside. The wind also produces “music” for the party to enjoy. The many winds that Heidi encounters sing and dance like performers, work like young farmers, and welcome guests like gracious hosts.

When Heidi goes to live with her grandfather in the mountains of Switzerland, she enters a new world. Suddenly, she is immersed in nature. Heidi begins to see aspects of the natural world more closely, in completely new ways. She comes to love the nature around her, and appreciates every detail. When Heidi looks at the mountains, she sees faces that look back at her. She considers them to be individuals with memories, able to forget and remember others. When Heidi goes to Germany and comes back, she feels that the mountains remember that she is their friend. Like the mountains, the sun can also communicate. Heidi makes sure to begin her days with a washed face so the sun does not laugh at her. She witnesses the sun say goodnight to the mountains, and bid her to wake and come outside in the morning. The various winds around Heidi also play an active role in her life. Some dance and sing. They perform in a way that always makes her stop to watch and listen. Other winds happily perform their springtime duties, and help the fir trees shed their old needles. Others help Heidi and her grandfather make their guests comfortable when they provide agreeable air and music. Heidi personifies the mountains, sun, and winds because they become so valuable to her.
