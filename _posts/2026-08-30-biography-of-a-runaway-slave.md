Esteban Montejo, the subject of Miguel Barnet’s 1968 _Biography of a Runaway Slave_, spends his entire life (from about 1868 to 1973) on the Caribbean island of Cuba. Despite Catholic Spain’s dominance over the island, however, Montejo never fully embraces the Catholic religion. Catholicism certainly influences Montejo, but it never consumes him. Other religions that also influence Montejo are the ones that enslaved African people bring from Africa to North America. Montejo describes religions such as Lucumí and the Conga, which come from regions in West and Central Africa. Montejo explains how his relationship to the various faiths depends largely on the people who communicate them to him. In Montejo’s Cuba, Catholicism is the religion of the white Spaniards. The priests dictate the rules and practices of the religion. Montejo feels disconnected from the priests because they live and work in very different spaces than most of the enslaved black people on the island. Furthermore, priesthood is not an option for black people. The priests avoid much of Montejo’s world and Montejo cannot access the world of the priests. Montejo also sees that the priests hold power over people on the island, and observes how they abuse that power. He knows of individual priests who take advantage of women and even father children, who swindle people out of money, and cause harm to those that displease them. The African religions, on the other hand, belong to a community of black people that he can more easily appreciate and trust. Esteban Montejo's religious views reflect the slavery based society in which he lives.

## Division

Montejo feels that an unbreachable divide exists between the black people in Cuba and the white Catholic priests on the island. Montejo relates, “I knew about two African religions in the barracoons, the Lucumí and the Conga…The other religion was Catholicism. It was introduced by the priests, who wouldn’t go into the barracoons during slavery for love or money…They had a serious look that didn’t sit well in the barracoons…Truth is, I never learned that doctrine because I did not understand it at all. I don’t think the house slaves did, either, but because they were so refined and so well treated, they became Christians” (pp. 22-23). Montejo explains that Catholicism is only one of many different religions in Cuba. The Lucumí and the Conga religions come from the African people that are enslaved alongside Montejo. These African people sleep in the same barracoon, or prison dormitory as Montejo. Because of Montejo’s physical closeness to the African people that practice the Lucumí and the Conga religions, he becomes familiar with them. In contrast, the priests that practice Catholicism do not go near the place where Montejo rests at night. The priests remain removed from the enslaved people and untrusted by them. Montejo argues that the enslaved people who work in closer proximity to the Spanish Catholics, the people who work in Spanish homes as opposed to the mills or fields, are more likely to adopt the Spanish Catholic ways. Just as the priests do not come near the enslaved people’s sleeping quarters, so too do they avoid the enslaved people’s work places. Montejo observes: “It seems to me that the matter of the trapiches didn’t attract [the priests’] attention. [The priests] never went up close to the machines. They were afraid of choking or going deaf. Nobody else was so delicate” (p. 63). Montejo believes the priests have no interest in the work of the enslaved people because the priests never visit the sugar mills and thus never see the work of the enslaved people there. Montejo claims the priests are unlike most people he knows in that they are too weak to stand the harsh environment of the sugar mill. The priests avoid the places where the enslaved people sleep and work, and do not allow any black people to enter into their sphere of life either. Montejo explains, “Priests and lawyers were sacred in that time. They were highly respected because of their titles... Blacks were none of these things, least of all priests. I never seen a black priest. That was for the whites, and the descendants of the Spaniards” (p. 60). Priests are set apart from others in society. The priests are more respected than most because the religion they practice is holy, and they themselves are holy. Black people do not have the opportunity to become priests. Montejo says he sees only white priests because that profession is reserved only for white people. The Catholic priests in Montejo’s world do not concern themselves with the daily lives of the enslaved black people, such as where they sleep and work, and black people are barred from the priesthood.

## Power

Montejo feels that the Catholic priests are not only apart from him, but also that they assert dominance over almost everyone. Montejo relates, “...I’ve never cared for priests. Some were criminals, even. They liked pretty white women and slept with them. They were both lecherous and pious. They had a child, and they called him a godchild or a nephew. They hid them under their robes…With women they were devils. They converted the sacristy into a whorehouse” (pp. 60, 62). Montejo explains that he does not like priests because he knows some that pretend to be chaste while they behave lewdly. Montejo says that when a Catholic priest fathers a child, the priest does not claim that child as his own. Instead, the father lies about his relationship to the child. The priest uses his priesthood to hide all of his unholy behavior. Montejo also alleges that the priests take money from people in a dishonest way. He says, “[Raffles] happened in Holy Week, and the religious people themselves encouraged it. Even today raffles are a monstrous fraud. And even more so among the priests... [one priest raffled] a bunch of junk. I realized that it was the same trick as before and that raffles continued to be tricks” (p. 114). Montejo describes the gambling competitions popular during the holiest week of the Christian year. He describes an instance in which a priest takes money from people and distributes worthless “prizes” in return. Montejo views the practice as a way for priests to take advantage of poor people. The priests maintain such power in the community that they can even decide who lives or dies. Montejo reveals, “The priests were influential in everything. When they said a black was ornery, you had to watch over him, because if you didn’t there was someone just waiting for the chance to get rid of him…There was a church close by, but I never went, because I knew very well that the real founders of the Inquisition in Cuba were the priests” (p. 62). If a priest feels threatened by someone he deems resistant to the status quo, he can turn others against the person. Montejo insists that if a priest expresses displeasure with a black person, people need to remain vigilant in their protection of the person, because one of the priests followers is sure to enact harm. Montejo also expresses his contempt for the Catholic Church’s intrusive efforts to discover and eliminate non-Catholics. The Catholic priests have a special control over women, money, and people’s very existence. 

## Relief 

In a society dominated by obscure and corruptible priests, the religions of the African people that live and work with Montejo feel safer. Montejo explains, “The fiestas in the Casas de Santería were very good. Only black people went there. The Spaniards didn’t approve of that. As the years went by things changed. Today you can see a white babalao priest with red cheeks. But it was different then, because Santería is an African religion” (p. 58). Montejo appreciates the festivities that take place in the black homes and communities that practice Santería. The celebratory gatherings feel safe to him because they are all black spaces. Montejo relates that Santería comes from the African population on the island, but is inclusive. Eventually, white people also adopt Santería and are able to become not only followers but also leaders. Montejo mentions a friend of his, a specific person that influences his life. He says, “The Fabás were both of them witches. One was called Lucas, and the other Ricardo or Regino. I was friends with Lucas…Lucas was a big witch…He was my only true friend” (pp. 66-67). Montejo doesn’t know any Catholic priests well, but he does know some witches well. Montejo describes the Fabás brothers as witches and his friends. Lucas’s beliefs and practices become familiar and endearing to Montejo because the two are so close. Montejo ultimately reflects, “...the truth is that I come to the conclusion that the African was wise in all things…They taught me many things though they did not know how to read or write. Customs that are more important than information. To be educated, not to meddle in other folks’ problems, speak softly, be respectful, be religious, be a hard worker…I respected them. A black Congo or Lucumi knew more about medicine than a doctor” (pp. 124-125). Montejo shares that the respected mentors in his life are not the Catholic priests, but the older people in his community that come from Africa. Born enslaved in Cuba, Montejo looks to the African-born elders to tell him about his own heritage outside of slavery. Montejo trusts the African elders to impart true wisdom to him, rather than biased propaganda that means to keep him docile in enslavement. Montejo embraces the African religions because they belong to his friends, respected elders, and the wider African community that accepts him.

_Biography of a Runaway Slave_ illustrates Esteban Montejo’s resistance to subjugation. As a young person, he escapes his enslavers and survives on his own in the wilderness. Later, he fights against Spanish rule in Cuba. Throughout his life, he also rejects the strict Catholicism that the colonizers of Cuba introduce. Montejo explains that Catholic priests do not go near the barracoons where he and the other enslaved people live. The priests also avoid some of the more difficult places where the enslaved people work such as the sugar mills. As the priests stay away from the harsh living and working places of the enslaved people, the two groups remain largely removed from each other. Furthermore, black people do not have the option to become priests. Not only does a division exist between the Catholic priests and the people of African descent on the island, the priests maintain positions of power in the society that allow them to abuse others. Montejo sees that priests are able to use women with impunity. He sees them enact bad faith schemes such as raffles to acquire money from an already poor population. Finally, Montejo highlights the power that priests have to call for violence against individuals that are not submissive to them. In this context of division and power imbalance on the island, Montejo understandably turns to his own community for safety. He welcomes the African religions he learns about from the all-black festivals, his friends, and elders. _Biography of a Runaway Slave_ helps explain why black people in Cuba develop their own religious traditions, ones that continue to endure today.
